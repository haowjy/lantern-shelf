A Letter in the Rain Gauge

The rain gauge at the observatory had not collected rain in seven months. It was a copper cylinder with a narrow mouth, fixed to a post in the garden. Mara emptied it each morning even when it held nothing. The ritual kept her honest about the weather, though Niko said it was also a way to make the empty vessel feel useful.

On Monday, there was a folded paper inside.

Mara shook the gauge gently. The paper did not come out. She used the thin end of her compass to ease it past the lip. It was wrapped in waxed cloth and tied with blue thread. The handwriting was unfamiliar, but the date on the outside was forty-three years old.

“Do you open it?” Niko asked from the doorway.

“It was left in the instrument.”

“That isn’t the same as being addressed to us.”

Mara set it on the desk. They left it there while she measured the morning wind. They left it through lunch. When the cloud gathered over the observatory, the paper’s blue thread lifted a fraction above the desk, though the windows were shut.

Mara untied it. The letter was from a child named Enna, who had written: “Dear whoever finds this, I think the island is too quiet in the west. The lighthouse stopped talking. My father says it’s only a machine, but the fog used to come home to it. I’m leaving this here for the weather to carry to my grandmother on the mainland. She lives in a place where it rains every day.”

There was no address, no family name, and no grandmother’s town. The letter ended with a drawing of a lighthouse and a tiny cloud with a smile.

Mara read it twice. The cloud above the roof brightened. A soft note came from the brass cage, and the paper lifted at one corner. She held it down with a stone.

“Maybe Enna left it in the gauge because she wanted the rain to take it,” Niko said. “Maybe it never did.”

The rain gauge’s inside was dry and clean. There was no way to know whether the letter had been there all those years or whether the cloud had carried it back from somewhere else. Mara recorded the date and the paper’s condition, then folded it along its old creases.

Sella recognized the name. “Enna lived above the old bakery. She moved away when she was twelve. Her grandmother did live on the mainland, in a town where the rain came sideways.”

“Do you know where she is now?”

Sella shook her head. “I can ask around.”

The island’s old letters were kept in a wooden cabinet at the town hall. People left them when a neighbor moved, or when a boat carried news to a distant port. Mara and Sella searched the cabinet. They found postcards, shopping lists, a recipe for plum jam, and a letter from Ione to the lighthouse keeper about a broken lantern wheel. There was no address for Enna.

That evening, Mara took the letter to the western headland. She placed it on a flat stone beside the brass cage. The cloud lowered until its edge touched the grass. A ribbon of mist moved around the paper, then drifted north, following the harbor’s hidden current.

They followed it to the old ferry steps, where a woman was unloading crates of apples from a small mainland boat. She introduced herself as Enna Vale. She was a weather archivist now, visiting Bellwether to collect records of the island’s fog seasons.

Mara handed her the letter. Enna stared at her childhood drawing. “I thought I had lost this. I used to slip notes into anything that collected rain.”

“The gauge brought it back,” Mara said, though she could not prove it.

Enna folded the paper carefully. “My grandmother got the first letter. She answered, but I never saw the reply. She wrote that she could hear the lighthouse in the rain. I thought she was being kind.”

They sat on the ferry steps. Enna described the mainland town: wet cobbles, low roofs, a river that ran through the market. Her grandmother had died years ago. The reply had stayed in a box of family papers until last spring, when Enna found it tucked inside a book of weather observations.

The cloud drifted above them, a small gray comma. It did not make a sound. The harbor seemed to listen for one anyway.

Mara asked permission to copy the letter into the observatory’s archive. Enna agreed. She added a note in the margin: “The lighthouse still talks. It just needs the island to answer.”

The next morning, a fine rain fell for four minutes over the western headland and nowhere else. The rain gauge caught enough water to cover its bottom. Mara measured it, wrote down the amount, and left the gauge where it was.
