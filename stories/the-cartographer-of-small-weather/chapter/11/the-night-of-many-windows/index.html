The Night of Many Windows

The night of the island’s lantern walk arrived with a clear sky and a mild wind. Every autumn, people carried small lights from the square to the lighthouse, remembering the keepers who had guided boats through fog. This year, Mara asked them to bring a window instead: a lantern with a pane of colored glass, or a jar with a candle inside.

The children arrived first, carrying paper boxes with cutout stars. The harbor families brought green bottles. Sella brought a square lamp made from two panes of the greenhouse’s old blue glass. Oren carried the buoy bell on a rope, although he had been told it was not a lantern.

“It shines when it rings,” he said.

Niko had wired a row of small lamps to the tide clock. They lit one at a time as the pendulum passed each mark. Mara stood in the square with a map and showed everyone the route: through the old market, along the drain, past the harbor bench, up to the lighthouse.

The walk began at dusk. Each person carried their light at a different height. The children’s stars bobbed above their heads. The glass jars made small pools of color on the stones. When the group reached the old market, the hidden current lifted the flames and made them lean toward the sea.

At the bench, people stopped. Some sat, some stood, and some turned toward the harbor as if expecting a boat. Oren rang the buoy bell once. The sound crossed the water and came back softer from the lighthouse.

The cloud appeared over the western headland. It was almost invisible against the evening sky, except for the silver line along its lower edge. It followed the lanterns up the hill, not touching them but catching their colors. The cloud’s seam reflected blue, green, gold, and red.

At the lighthouse, Luma took the old wheel. She turned it one notch, then stepped away. The glass in the lantern room scattered the lights across the stones. For a moment, the island had many small windows facing outward.

Mara looked at her map. The dotted paths she had drawn—air, water, sound, waiting—seemed to meet at the tower. The lights did not make a single beam. Each showed a small part of the night, and together they made the whole route visible.

A child asked if the cloud could see them.

“I think it can feel the warmth,” Mara said. “But seeing may not be the word.”

“Then what word?”

“Noticing.”

The cloud drifted over the crowd. No one tried to catch it. The lanterns warmed the air, and the old wheel turned slowly under Luma’s hand. The cloud gathered above the tower, then spread like a thin roof. A few drops of moisture fell on the grass. They caught the lantern light without becoming rain.

The island’s bells began to ring, one by one. The buoy, the tide clock, the greenhouse chime, and the lighthouse wheel each gave their own note. The sounds did not line up neatly. They overlapped, separated, and found each other again. People listened in the same quiet way they listened for a kettle to boil.

Mara’s map fluttered in her hands. She had meant to mark the route, but the wind turned the page and showed the blank side. She held it against the lantern room’s wall. A soft shadow of the cloud crossed it, moving from the headland toward the harbor.

She drew a line where the shadow passed. Then another, for the lanterns. The map looked less like a chart and more like a conversation recorded from one seat at the table.

When the walk ended, everyone returned to the square. They left the lights on the bakery steps so the late boats could see the shore. Sella gave out warm apple cakes. Oren hung the buoy bell by the door. The children carried their paper stars home, their cutout windows shining with candlelight.

Mara stayed at the lighthouse until the last lantern went dark. The cloud was already moving out over the water. It did not vanish; it became part of the night’s pale haze.

She closed her notebook, then opened it again to write one final line: “A window is a way to share light without keeping it.”

The cloud moved toward the sea, and the island left its windows open.
